GaugePerforationsAbout
Regular 8mmOne edge, larger holes at the frame linesNarrow film with sprocket holes down one side and small, nearly square frames. Kodak introduced it in 1932 as an affordable home format: 16mm stock was exposed down one half, flipped and run through again, then slit down the middle to yield two 8mm strips. It dominated home movies from the 1930s into the 1960s and usually turns up on 3-inch (50 ft) reels.
Super 8One edge, smaller holes centered on each frameThe same 8mm width as Regular 8, but with much smaller perforations that freed up room for a frame about 50 percent larger. Kodak launched it in 1965 in an easy-load plastic cartridge, which is often the giveaway. It became the standard home-movie and student format through the 1970s, and many later cartridges carried a magnetic sound stripe.
16mmOne or both edgesTwice the width of 8mm, introduced by Kodak in 1923 as a safer, cheaper alternative to 35mm. Double-perforated 16mm (both edges) is normally silent. Single-perforated film may give up one row for an optical or magnetic soundtrack, but single-perf stock is also widely used for camera originals and is required for Super 16, so a single row alone does not prove sound. It was long the workhorse of documentary, newsreel, educational, and industrial filmmaking.
Super 16One edge onlyStandard 16mm stock exposed with a single row of perforations, letting the frame extend into the space the second row would use for a wider image of roughly 1.66. It is a camera origination format rather than a projection one, meant to be blown up to 35mm or finished for widescreen. A single perf row plus a frame reaching almost to the far edge is the tell.
35mmBoth edges; commonly four holes per frameThe theatrical standard since the birth of cinema, with perforations down both edges, most commonly four per frame. Reduced pulldowns of 2-perf and 3-perf also exist, and VistaVision runs the film horizontally at eight perforations per frame. It is exactly the same width as a 35mm still-photography negative, because motion-picture film came first and the still format borrowed the stock. Its large frame and a century of infrastructure make it the benchmark other gauges are measured against.

Date your Kodak film by its edge code

For most of the twentieth century, Eastman Kodak printed a small symbol code along the edge of its motion-picture film, outside the perforations, to mark the year the stock was made. The symbols differ by the plant that coated the film, so choose the plant first. Then read the symbols left to right and pick them below, filling only as many as your film shows. This is the year the film was manufactured, not necessarily when it was shot or processed.

    Film from 1989 on: the two-letter KEYKODE

    From 1989, Kodak KEYKODE edge print carries two-letter codes. One is the year of manufacture, printed at the end after EASTMAN (for example EN = 2008). Another identifies the film stock by its Kodak catalog number. Look up either below. The same two letters can mean different things in the two systems, so read the code from the matching spot on the edge. (Since 2003, many 35mm prints show the full four-digit year, so no year lookup is needed.)

     
     

    Some Kodak film made from 2008 on carries a multi-year numeric stencil instead of a two-letter code, for example a run of years like 12 11 10 9 8. The last (lowest) number in the sequence is the year of manufacture, so that example is 2008.

    Factory ID code: where the film was made

    Alongside the date symbols, Kodak marked the plant that coated the film with a single dot placed between two letters, and the dot appears in both KODAK and SAFETY. The chart below shows the common SAFETY dot positions as a simplified quick reference. A full identification reads both words together with the dot's vertical placement: the two US plants, Rochester, New York and Windsor, Colorado, share the same SAFETY dot and are told apart only by the dot in KODAK and its height. Kodak began marking Rochester around 1927 and the overseas plants around 1936; some positions here, such as Australia, come from older preservation charts rather than Kodak's current list. For a definitive result, compare against Kodak's published plant-of-origin chart.

    Aspect ratios

    The aspect ratios you are most likely to meet in a film collection, from silent Academy to modern anamorphic scope.

    RatioNameAboutShape
    1.19:1MovietoneOne of the first sound-on-film formats, introduced by Fox in the late 1920s. Fitting an optical soundtrack beside the picture narrowed the frame to a nearly square shape, a look that lasted only until the Academy ratio standardized the sound-era frame.
    1.33:1Silent / full aperture (4:3)The classic 4:3 rectangle. It fills the full 35mm camera aperture of the silent era and is the native shape of Regular 8, Super 8, and 16mm film, as well as pre-widescreen television.
    1.37:1AcademyStandardized by the Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences in 1932. To make room for an optical soundtrack running alongside the picture, the image area was reduced, and the frame was then reshaped into a balanced rectangle. It stayed the theatrical norm until widescreen arrived in the 1950s.
    1.43:1IMAXThe largest common film format. 65/70mm stock runs sideways through the camera rather than vertically, each frame spanning 15 perforations, producing a negative roughly ten times the area of a standard 35mm frame.
    1.66:1European widescreenA modest widescreen that became a common projection standard across Europe from the 1950s onward. It is also the native shape of Super 16, often used to originate material for wider finishing.
    1.78:116:9The shape of HD and modern digital video and displays. It sits between 1.66 and 1.85, chosen as a compromise widescreen for presenting both older and theatrical material on television.
    1.85:1US widescreen (flat)The standard American "flat" widescreen since the mid-1950s. It is created by masking the top and bottom of a full 35mm frame in projection, rather than with an anamorphic lens.
    2.20:170mmThe presentation ratio of traditional 5-perf 70mm roadshow films. The wide frame and large negative made it the premium large-format experience of its era.
    2.35:1CinemaScope / early scopeThe classic anamorphic widescreen, horizontally squeezed onto 35mm by a special lens and unsqueezed in projection. CinemaScope debuted in 1953 at about 2.55:1 with magnetic sound; 2.35:1 became the standard soon after to make room for an optical soundtrack, and it held until the anamorphic spec was slightly revised in 1970.
    2.39:1Modern anamorphic (scope)The current anamorphic "scope" standard, a 1970 refinement of the earlier 2.35 that made frame-line splices less visible. It remains the widest ratio in everyday theatrical use.
